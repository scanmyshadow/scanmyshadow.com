How to Check What Personal Information About You Is Public Online

Scan My Shadow · August 26, 2026
PrivacyCyber SecurityIndiaData BreachPersonal Information

TL;DR: Run the same searches a stranger would run on you — search engines, image search, people-search sites, and platform-specific lookups — to see exactly what's publicly visible right now, then tighten what you find.

There's a difference between "is my data leaked somewhere" and "what would a stranger actually see if they looked me up right now." The first is about breaches and dark corners of the internet. The second is about what's sitting in plain sight — searchable, visible, no special tools required. This is that second check, done properly.

Search yourself the way a stranger would

Open an incognito/private browser window — logged out, no personalization skewing results — and search your full name in quotes, then your name plus your city, then your name plus your employer. Go through the first three pages of results, not just the first five links. Public information often sits past the first page: an old comment on a news article, a college fest registration list still indexed, a business directory entry.

Run an image search on yourself

Search engines let you search by image, not just text. Upload a recent photo of yourself and see where else it turns up — this catches things a text search misses, like a photo reused on a site you never gave permission to, or an old profile picture still indexed somewhere you forgot existed.

Check what's visible on your own social profiles — logged out

Visit your own Facebook, Instagram, and LinkedIn profiles while logged out, or from a different account entirely. What you see logged out is what a stranger sees. It's common to be surprised here — a phone number field set to "public" without remembering, a full friends list visible, old posts that were never actually private.

Search your phone number and email specifically

Put your phone number and email into a search engine in quotes, separately. This catches business listings, marketplace posts (a secondhand item you once listed for sale), forum sign-ups, and any site where you entered contact details for something one-off years ago.

Check people-search and directory-style sites directly

Beyond general search engines, some sites exist specifically to aggregate and display personal information in profile form — compiling whatever public or semi-public data they can find into one page. Searching your name directly on a few of these will show you whether you have a profile, and roughly what's on it.

Check what your workplace or past employers have published

Company "About Us" or team pages, old press releases, event speaker lists, and conference bios often stay online long after you've moved on. These are usually harmless, but worth knowing about — particularly if they include contact details or information you'd rather not have surfacing years later.

Build a simple list as you go

Don't just browse — note down every URL where you find something, with a rough note on what it shows (name only, name + number, old photo, etc.). This turns a vague "I should check this sometime" into an actual, workable list you can act on.

What to do with what you find

  • Tighten privacy settings on anything social-media related that's more visible than you intended.
  • Request removal from people-search or directory sites that have a profile on you — most offer an opt-out form, even if it takes some digging to find.
  • Ask sites you don't control (an old employer's page, a forum) to take down or update anything outdated, especially if it includes contact details.
  • Leave low-risk items alone — not everything needs action. A five-year-old comment on a news article with just your first name isn't worth chasing down.

What this manual check won't catch

This method covers what's publicly indexed and visible through ordinary search — it doesn't cover data broker databases that aren't search-engine indexed, breach dumps circulating privately, or listings on sites that block search engine crawlers. FAQs

How often should I run this kind of self-check?

Once or twice a year is reasonable for most people, or after a major life change — new job, house move, or public event — since those tend to generate fresh public mentions.

Is it normal to find things I don't remember posting?

Yes — old accounts, forgotten forum posts, and sites that indexed content years ago are common. It's less a sign of a specific problem and more a normal byproduct of having used the internet for years.

Can I remove myself from search engine results directly?

Search engines generally reflect what's publicly hosted elsewhere — to actually remove something, you usually need to get it taken down or made private at the source, after which it drops out of search results over time.
