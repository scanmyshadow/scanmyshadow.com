Where Your Phone Number and Email Come From Online

Your contact information appears online through multiple channels, most of which you have limited control over. Understanding these sources is the first step toward protecting your privacy.

Data Breaches at Companies You've Worked With

When an unauthorized party gains access to a company's database and extracts user records, if your email address is part of that database—which it almost always is—it gets swept up in the breach, whether you did anything wrong or not. Breaches happen regularly across e-commerce platforms, social networks, financial apps, and forums. Data breach incidents include the personal information of millions of users, including 6 million users in India. A phone number appears in leaked datasets from Indian websites; a breach could pair your phone number with usernames, email addresses, or names—for example, if you registered on a forum that was later compromised, a breach database might link your phone to an email.

Data Brokers and Legal Information Trading

Data brokers are companies that legally collect and sell personal information. Companies known as data brokers quietly collect information, piece it together, and sell it, with the buyer possibly being an advertiser, an insurance company, or even someone you would not want holding your details. In India, this industry has grown significantly. Data broker spreadsheets contain personal details such as name, email, phone number, industry, and address—the selling price for details on 10,000 high net worth individuals from Bengaluru was Rs 2,000, and one lakh contacts would cost just Rs 7,000.

Telecom companies in India are legally required to collect identity documents during SIM registration under mandatory know your customer rules, building enormous databases that link phone numbers to names, addresses, and Aadhaar numbers. This data can be leaked through employees or sold by corrupt individuals within organizations.

Public Records and Online Platforms

Data brokers sell user information obtained from third-party service providers in the e-commerce, telecom, hospitality, retail and digital media sectors. When you register on classifieds sites, property portals, or e-commerce platforms, your phone number and email often remain in searchable databases. On classifieds platforms your phone numbers and location data remain accessible even after ads expire, and on India's largest classified ads platform, phone numbers shared with buyers remain in OLX databases and search engine caches.

Social Media and Public Sharing

Personal information shared on social media, forums, or public profiles can be scraped and aggregated by automated tools. Even if you delete a profile, the information may be cached by search engines or captured by data-collection services.

What to Do If You Find Your Information Is Exposed

If Your Email Was in a Breach

  • Change your password immediately for the affected service and any other accounts using the same password
  • Enable two-factor authentication on critical accounts like email, banking, and social media
  • Monitor your accounts for suspicious activity over the next weeks
  • Set up breach alerts to get notified if your email appears in future breaches

If Your Phone Number Appears in Breaches or Data Broker Databases

  • Be cautious of unsolicited calls and texts—scammers often use exposed phone numbers for targeted fraud
  • Do not answer calls from unknown numbers claiming to need verification information
  • Use call-blocking apps like Truecaller to reduce spam and scam calls
  • Report spam and phishing attempts to relevant authorities

Long-Term Privacy Protection Strategies

Limit What You Share Online

  • Avoid posting your phone number on public profiles or classifieds unless necessary
  • Use separate email addresses for different purposes (one for shopping, one for newsletters, one for banking)
  • Be selective about forms and sign-ups—only provide information when absolutely required

Understand Your Rights Under Privacy Law

The Digital Personal Data Protection Act emphasizes data localization and cross-border data transfers which may lead to increased investments in data infrastructure within the country, as companies will have to store and process Indian users' personal data within India's borders. Under the DPDP Act 2023, you have the right to request deletion or removal of your data from certain platforms. However, enforcement is ongoing, and not all companies comply immediately.

Monitor Your Accounts Regularly

  • Check credit reports and banking statements for unauthorized activity
  • Review privacy settings on all social media accounts twice a year
  • Run an email exposure check at least twice yearly to catch new breaches

FAQs About Phone and Email Exposure

1. Is it illegal for data brokers to sell my phone number?

In most cases, data brokers operate legally in India because there is limited regulation specifically governing data brokerage. Privacy laws are not as strict as in Europe under GDPR, which means brokers can gather and sell large amounts of data with very little oversight. The DPDP Act 2023 provides some protections, but enforcement is still developing. However, selling data obtained through breaches or without proper authorization is illegal.

2. How do I remove my information from data brokers?

Some data brokers offer opt-out pages where you can request removal. Some brokers provide opt-out pages where you can fill a request form and may ask for ID proof to make sure it's really you, and a few days later your profile may be removed from their list. However, while this will remove your personal info from the site in the short term, you may find your info back on the same data broker site in the future because many state laws only require one-time removal of existing records, and if a broker collects new data like a new address or phone number, it may be treated as a fresh record not covered by your original request. Removal requests require persistence and follow-up.

3. What is the most common way phone numbers get exposed?

The most common exposure sources are data breaches at services where you registered, data brokers purchasing information from e-commerce or telecom providers, and public classifieds or social media where you shared your number. Data brokers sell user information obtained from third-party service providers in the e-commerce, telecom, hospitality, retail and digital media sectors.

4. Can I sue a company if my data was breached?

Under Indian law, your options are limited but growing. The DPDP Act 2023 provides a complaint mechanism, and you may file complaints with the Data Protection Board. However, class-action lawsuits for data breaches are rare in India. Your best protection is early detection through tools like exposure checkers and timely action (password changes, fraud alerts) once you know your data has been exposed.

5. How often should I check if my information is exposed?

Security experts recommend checking at least once every 6 months, or immediately after major breaches in the news. If you use your email for many online services or regularly create new accounts, checking quarterly is prudent. Running a check takes only a few minutes and can reveal exposure you were never notified about.
