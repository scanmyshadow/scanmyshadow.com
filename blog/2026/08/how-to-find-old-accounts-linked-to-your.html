How to Find Old Accounts Linked to Your Phone Number

Scan My ShadowAugust 26, 20264 min read

TL;DR: Most people have far more old, forgotten accounts than they'd guess — here's a practical method for actually finding them, instead of trying to remember from scratch.

Try to list every account tied to your phone number right now, from memory. Most people get to eight or ten and stall. The real number is usually two or three times that — old sign-ups, one-time purchases, apps tried once and abandoned. The good news is you don't have to rely on memory. There's a systematic way to surface most of them.

Method 1: Search your email inbox

Search your email for terms like "verify your number," "OTP," "welcome," or the specific digits of your phone number. Account creation emails, verification confirmations, and welcome messages tend to surface a long trail of services you signed up for and forgot about — this is usually the single most productive method.

Method 2: Check your SMS history

If you haven't cleared it in years, your SMS inbox is a direct record of every OTP you've ever received — and each one corresponds to an account creation or login somewhere. Scroll back as far as it goes; sender IDs (the short alphanumeric codes before each message) will hint at which service sent it.

Method 3: Use the "forgot password" trick deliberately

For any service you vaguely remember signing up for but can't confirm, go to its login page and try "forgot password" or "log in with phone number." If an account exists, the system will usually confirm it by sending an OTP or reset link — telling you definitively whether that old account is still live, without you needing to remember your original credentials.

Method 4: Check your password manager

If you use a password manager (or your browser's built-in one), it likely has a saved list of every site you've ever logged into with autofill — often stretching back years further than your own memory does.

Method 5: Check app store purchase and download history

Both Google Play and the Apple App Store keep a record of every app you've ever installed, even ones you deleted long ago. Scrolling through this list is often the fastest way to jog memory about smaller or niche apps you'd otherwise never think of.

Method 6: Check linked social logins

If you regularly used "Sign in with Google" or "Sign in with Facebook" to skip creating separate passwords, both platforms let you see a list of connected third-party apps in your account security settings — often revealing services you'd completely forgotten you'd linked.

What to do once you've found them

  • Actually use them, or delete the account. Don't just leave them dormant — dormant accounts are exactly the kind that get breached and forgotten simultaneously.
  • Update passwords on anything you're keeping, particularly if it was set years ago and may be reused elsewhere.
  • Note which ones had your address or payment details saved — these carry more weight than simple sign-ups when deciding what to prioritize deleting.

Why this exercise is worth doing periodically

Each old, forgotten account is a place your phone number or email sits, outside your active awareness, in a database you have no visibility into. Doing this cleanup once doesn't make it permanent — new accounts accumulate again over time, which is why pairing manual cleanup with an occasional broader exposure check is more effective than either alone. FAQs

What if I can't remember the email or number I used to sign up years ago?

Try any old emails or numbers you've historically used, not just your current ones — many old accounts are tied to a previous phone number or a since-abandoned email address.

Is it worth deleting an old account if I've forgotten the password entirely?

Yes — most services support account recovery via email or phone OTP even without the original password, which is usually enough to log in and initiate deletion.

Should I worry about accounts I genuinely can't recover access to?

It's a reasonable low-level concern rather than an urgent one — if you can't regain access, focus instead on ensuring your current, active accounts use strong, unique passwords so an old inaccessible account can't compromise them.
