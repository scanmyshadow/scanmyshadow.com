Introduction: Why Your Personal Information May Already Be Out There

Most of us share our phone number and email address with dozens of services over the years — shopping apps, food delivery platforms, job portals, news subscriptions, social media profiles, and more. Each sign-up adds another entry point where your personal information may be stored, indexed, or in some cases, publicly visible.

The problem is not limited to any one type of service. Over time, old accounts accumulate, data breaches occur, and information shared years ago can resurface in unexpected ways. For Indian users in particular, the rapid growth of digital services has made this an increasingly relevant concern. India's growing internet population and the rise of large-scale data incidents affecting millions of users have made personal data exposure a real and practical risk — not just a theoretical one.

The good news is that checking your exposure is possible, and taking a few focused steps can meaningfully reduce the amount of your personal information that is publicly accessible.

How Does Personal Information End Up Online?

1. Data Breaches

When a company or service you use suffers a security incident, personal data stored in their systems — including email addresses, phone numbers, and in some cases passwords — can be exposed. Indian users have been affected by several significant breaches in recent years. For example, in February 2024, a breach at Indian electronics brand boAt reportedly exposed the personal data of over 7.5 million customers, including names, addresses, phone numbers, and email addresses. A separate incident at Hathway, a major Indian ISP, reportedly compromised the personal information of over 41.5 million customers.

Breached data often circulates in places that are difficult for ordinary users to monitor. Even if you changed your password after an incident, your email address or phone number may still be in circulation and could be used to build more convincing targeted scams against you.

2. Old and Forgotten Online Accounts

It is easy to accumulate accounts over the years — on forums, e-commerce sites, job portals, or apps that you no longer use. An old account may continue to retain personal information even after you stop using a service, depending on the platform's data-retention practices. These dormant accounts can also be more vulnerable because they often use older, weaker passwords and may lack up-to-date security settings.

As security researchers note, forgotten accounts often contain old passwords, outdated recovery details, or personal data that can be exploited if the account is ever compromised. Regularly reviewing and deleting accounts you no longer need is one of the simplest privacy habits you can adopt.

3. Publicly Visible Profiles and Directories

Some personal information becomes publicly accessible not through breaches, but through choices made at sign-up. Social media profiles with public contact details, professional directories, and classified listing sites can all make your phone number or email visible to anyone who searches for them. In some cases, data aggregation services pull together information from multiple public sources, making it easier for third parties to build a profile on an individual.

4. Third-Party App Permissions

When apps request permission to access your contacts, location, or other data, that information may be shared with advertising partners or data processing services. While Indian privacy law is evolving — the Digital Personal Data Protection (DPDP) Act, 2023 and its Rules, formally notified in November 2025, establish a consent-based framework for how personal data should be collected and used — individual users still benefit from being mindful of what permissions they grant.

What Information Is Most Commonly Exposed?

  • Email addresses: Used as a primary identifier across most online services. Frequently appear in breach databases and in aggregated data sets.
  • Phone numbers: Used for OTP verification, account recovery, and identity checks across banking, UPI, and social media. A phone number linked to many services becomes a sensitive identifier.
  • Names and addresses: Often shared when shopping, ordering food, or booking services. Can persist in old accounts or be exposed in breaches affecting e-commerce and delivery platforms.
  • Passwords (hashed or plain text): When exposed, they can enable credential-stuffing attacks, where attackers try the same username-password combination across multiple sites.

It is worth noting that even partial information — a phone number paired with a name, for instance — can make targeted fraud more convincing. As one recent Business Standard article noted, when cyber criminals already know your name and personal details, they can craft far more convincing phishing messages or impersonate bank officials to trick you into revealing sensitive credentials.

You can also check your email exposure and phone number exposure separately, depending on which identifier you're most concerned about.

Step 2: Search for Yourself Online

Search your full name, phone number, and email address in quotes on major search engines. Check image results as well. Look for your name on professional directories, old forum posts, classifieds, and social media platforms you may have forgotten about. This gives you a broad sense of what a stranger could find about you with minimal effort.

Step 3: Review Your Active Social Media Privacy Settings

On each platform you actively use, check who can see your contact details, your posts, and your profile information. Many platforms default to broader visibility settings than users realise. Restricting your phone number and email address from public view on platforms like LinkedIn, Facebook, Instagram, and WhatsApp is a straightforward step that reduces passive exposure.

Step 4: Audit Your Old Accounts

Search your email inbox for terms like "welcome", "verify your email", or "confirm your account" to surface sign-up emails from services you may have forgotten. For each service you identify but no longer use, consider whether to delete the account entirely or at minimum update the personal information stored there. Where possible, submit a formal account deletion request rather than simply deactivating.

Step 5: Check for Your Information on Data Aggregation Sites

Some sites compile personal information from public records and other sources. Searching your name and phone number on such platforms can reveal whether your contact details are publicly listed. If you find your information, most platforms have an opt-out or removal process, though the steps and timelines vary by service.

Practical Steps to Reduce Your Digital Exposure Going Forward

  • Use a secondary email address for sign-ups. Keeping a dedicated email for app registrations and subscriptions limits the exposure of your primary email address and makes it easier to manage unwanted contact.
  • Be selective about sharing your phone number. Not every service needs your real phone number. Where a phone number is optional, consider leaving it out, or using a secondary number for less critical services.
  • Enable two-factor authentication (2FA) on important accounts. Even if your password is exposed in a breach, 2FA adds an additional layer of protection.
  • Review app permissions regularly. On Android and iOS, periodically audit which apps have access to your contacts, location, microphone, and camera. Revoke permissions that are not necessary for the app's core function.
  • Delete accounts you no longer use. A smaller number of active accounts means fewer places where your personal information can be stored or exposed.
  • Avoid reusing passwords. If one service is breached, reused passwords can give attackers access to your other accounts. A password manager can help you maintain unique passwords without needing to memorise them.
  • Periodically re-check your exposure. Your digital footprint changes over time as new breaches occur and old data resurfaces. Running a periodic check on your email and phone number helps you stay informed.

India's Evolving Data Protection Framework

India's approach to personal data privacy has been strengthening in recent years. The Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 (DPDP Act) established a legal framework for personal data protection, and the DPDP Rules, 2025, formally notified in November 2025, provide the operational requirements for implementing the Act. The Data Protection Board of India, established on 13 November 2025, is now active and will adjudicate disputes between individuals and organisations under the framework. Full compliance obligations for organisations are phased, with key requirements expected to apply by May 2027.

For individual users, these developments are meaningful — they establish formal rights to seek information about how your data is used and to request corrections or erasure in certain circumstances. However, regulatory frameworks take time to become fully effective, and the most practical step you can take right now is to understand your own exposure and take proactive measures to protect your information.

How do I find out if my phone number is being circulated online?

You can search your phone number in quotes on major search engines to see if it appears in any public listings or directories. Can my personal information be online even if I have not been hacked?

Yes. Your personal information can appear online through several routes that do not involve your own accounts being directly hacked. These include data breaches at companies whose services you have used, publicly visible profiles on platforms you signed up for, old accounts that retain your details, and data aggregation sites that compile information from public records. Exposure does not necessarily mean your accounts have been compromised, but it can increase the risk of targeted fraud or spam.

What is the difference between a data breach and general digital exposure?

A data breach refers specifically to an incident where an unauthorised party gains access to personal data held by an organisation. General digital exposure is broader and includes any way in which your personal information may be visible or accessible online — including through your own public profiles, old accounts, directories, and aggregated public records. Both types of exposure are worth understanding and checking.

Is it safe to use a tool that checks my phone number and email for exposure?

Reputable exposure-checking tools work by searching publicly available or breach-database sources — they do not require access to your accounts. Scan My Shadow, for instance, checks submitted phone numbers and email addresses across available sources to produce an exposure report. As with any online service, it is sensible to review the privacy policy before submitting personal information and to choose services with a clear explanation of how your data is handled.

What should I do if I find my personal information has been exposed in a breach?

If your email or phone number appears in a known breach, the recommended steps include changing the password for the affected service (and any other services where you used the same password), enabling two-factor authentication on important accounts, being extra cautious about unsolicited calls or messages that claim to be from banks or government bodies, and monitoring your linked accounts for unusual activity. If your phone suddenly loses network connectivity unexpectedly, contact your telecom operator promptly, as fraudsters sometimes obtain duplicate SIM cards to intercept OTPs.
