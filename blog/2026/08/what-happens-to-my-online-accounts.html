What Happens to My Online Accounts After I Die?

Scan My Shadow · August 26, 2026
PrivacyCyber SecurityIndiaAccount Safety

TL;DR: Most accounts don't automatically close when you die — they sit dormant, sometimes indefinitely, unless someone with the right access or authority deliberately closes or transfers them. Here's what actually happens, and how to plan for it.

The default outcome: nothing happens automatically

Unless a platform is specifically told, most online accounts simply continue existing after someone dies — social media profiles remain visible, subscriptions may keep auto-renewing on a linked card, and email accounts sit untouched, sometimes for years, unless a family member takes deliberate action.

What some platforms offer specifically for this

A number of major platforms have built specific processes for this situation — memorialization features that convert a profile into a fixed tribute state, or account-closure requests that a verified family member can submit with a death certificate. These aren't automatic; someone has to know they exist and actively initiate the process.

The DPDP Act's nomination right

India's DPDP Act includes a distinctive provision — the right to nominate another person to exercise your data rights on your behalf in the event of your death or incapacity. This is a genuinely useful, India-specific mechanism: rather than family members having to navigate each individual platform's own process from scratch, a formally nominated person has a legally recognized basis to act on your data rights directly.

Financial and identity-linked accounts need separate handling

Banking, UPI, and Aadhaar-linked accounts follow entirely separate processes from social media, generally requiring a death certificate and legal heir documentation submitted directly to the bank or through formal estate processes — not something resolved through a platform's general account-closure form.

Why leaving this unaddressed creates real risk

A dormant, unmanaged account isn't just an emotional loose end for family — it's an ongoing exposure risk. An old email account with a weak, unchanged password, still holding years of financial and personal correspondence, remains a live target for compromise indefinitely if nobody closes or secures it. This is a genuine, practical reason to plan for this deliberately, beyond the more obvious emotional considerations.

A practical checklist for planning ahead

  • Use a password manager with an emergency access or legacy contact feature, if available, so a trusted person can access your accounts if needed.
  • Consider using the nomination feature where platforms and services (including under the DPDP Act) offer it, formally designating who should have authority over your accounts.
  • Keep a simple, updated list of your major accounts and financial services for whoever would need to handle your affairs — doesn't need to include passwords directly, just enough for someone to know where to start.
  • Talk to family about your preferences — whether you'd want accounts memorialized, closed, or left as-is — since this is ultimately a personal decision worth stating clearly rather than leaving to guesswork.

For family members handling this after a loss

If you're managing a loved one's accounts after their passing, start with the accounts most likely to pose an actual risk if left open — primary email (since it's usually the recovery point for everything else) and any financial or payment apps — before working through less urgent social accounts. Most major platforms have a dedicated, documented process for this; searching "[platform name] deceased user account" typically surfaces the specific steps required.

How this connects to ongoing digital security more broadly

Planning for this isn't just an end-of-life consideration — the same habits (knowing what accounts exist, keeping access organized, understanding your legal rights over your own data) are the same ones that reduce your everyday exposure while you're actively using these accounts. FAQs

Can a family member access a deceased person's email without their password?

Generally not directly, without going through the email provider's specific deceased-user process, which typically requires documentation like a death certificate and proof of relationship or legal authority.

Does the DPDP Act's nomination feature need to be set up in advance?

Yes — it's a proactive designation you make while account rights are still yours to assign, not something that can be arranged retroactively after death.

What happens to auto-renewing subscriptions on a deceased person's card?

They typically continue charging until the card is cancelled or the subscription is actively stopped — worth checking and addressing relatively early in the process to avoid ongoing, unnecessary charges.
