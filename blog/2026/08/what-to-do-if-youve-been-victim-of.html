What to Do If You’ve Been a Victim of Matrimonial Fraud

Scan My Shadow · August 26, 2026
PrivacyCyber SecurityIndiaMatrimonial Safety

TL;DR: Acting quickly and in the right order — reporting, securing your finances, and leaning on support rather than isolating — meaningfully improves both recovery odds and how you come through this. Here's the specific path forward.

If you're reading this because it's already happened, the most important thing to know first: this is a documented, common crime pattern, not a personal failure. Matrimonial fraud is built specifically to exploit trust and emotional investment — falling for it reflects the sophistication of the scam, not a lack of judgment on your part.

Step 1: Stop all further contact and financial engagement immediately

The moment you recognize fraud, cut off further communication and, critically, don't send any additional money — even if there's pressure framed as "one last payment to fix everything." This pressure to send more is itself a common continuation of the scam, exploiting the sunk-cost feeling of having already invested money or trust.

Step 2: Report to your bank immediately if money was sent

Call your bank's fraud helpline directly and explicitly report it as an unauthorized or fraudulent transaction, requesting a freeze or dispute be logged. The faster this happens, the better the odds funds can still be traced or held before moving further through the banking chain.

Step 3: File with 1930 and cybercrime.gov.in without delay

Call the National Cyber Crime Helpline (1930) — available 24/7 and built specifically for financial fraud — then complete the formal complaint on cybercrime.gov.in within 24 hours using the acknowledgment number from your call. Gather what you have: transaction IDs, screenshots of conversations, the profile details, and any payment confirmations, since these strengthen the case significantly.

Step 4: Report the profile directly to the matrimonial platform

Separately from the cybercrime process, report the fraudulent profile to the platform itself so it can be investigated and taken down — this protects other users from the same scheme and creates an additional record of the incident.

Step 5: File a police complaint if warranted

Depending on the scale and nature of the fraud, filing an FIR at your local police station or through your district's cyber cell may be appropriate alongside the cybercrime portal complaint — particularly for larger financial losses or cases involving identity concealment that led to an actual marriage under false pretenses.

If the fraud involved an actual marriage, not just courtship

Cases where identity concealment (an undisclosed existing marriage, misrepresented facts) led to an actual wedding carry additional legal dimensions — these can potentially be grounds for annulment or specific fraud-related legal action, distinct from the standard cybercrime process. This is a situation worth consulting a family law practitioner about directly, given how much the right path depends on your specific circumstances.

Step 6: Tell your family, even though it feels difficult

The isolation these scams often create — encouraging secrecy from family — makes disclosure feel harder than it should. But involving trusted family members isn't just emotionally important; practically, they can help with the reporting process, provide a second perspective, and prevent the isolation the scam relied on from continuing after the fact.

Dealing with the emotional impact

Matrimonial fraud victims commonly experience real emotional trauma — shame, betrayal, and a period of withdrawal from social circles are documented, common reactions, not an overreaction. This is a legitimate loss worth processing, and there's no requirement to minimize how it's affected you simply because the relationship wasn't "real" in the way you believed.

Protecting yourself going forward

  • Change any passwords or account details you may have shared during the relationship, even indirectly.
  • Watch for follow-up scams — victims of one fraud are sometimes specifically targeted again with "recovery service" scams promising to help get the money back for an upfront fee.
  • Take time before re-engaging with matrimonial platforms — there's no obligation to jump back in immediately, and a verification-first approach (covered in our verification guide) is worth adopting deliberately next time.

Checking what else may have been exposed

Beyond the direct financial loss, it's worth checking whether your phone number or email — shared during the relationship — show any signs of broader exposure, since scammers sometimes reuse or resell contact information gathered during these schemes. FAQs

Is it possible to recover money sent to a matrimonial scammer?

Recovery isn't guaranteed, but reporting quickly through 1930 and cybercrime.gov.in significantly improves the odds if funds haven't yet moved through multiple accounts.

Should I confront the scammer directly before reporting?

Generally not recommended — it can prompt them to disappear or destroy evidence before you've had a chance to document and report properly. Report first, through official channels.

Will everyone find out if I report this?

No — calls to 1930 are handled confidentially, and cybercrime complaints aren't publicly disclosed. Choosing to tell family is a personal decision separate from the reporting process itself.
