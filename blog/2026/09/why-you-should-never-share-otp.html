Why You Should Never Share an OTP — Even With "Your Bank"

Scan My Shadow · September 3, 2026

TL;DR: An OTP exists to prove that you, specifically, are approving a specific action — which is exactly why nearly every scam script, regardless of how it starts, ends with a request for one. No bank, government agency, or delivery service legitimately needs you to read an OTP aloud or forward it to anyone.

KYC scams, government scheme fraud, the "guess who" WhatsApp scam, fake parcel calls — nearly every major scam covered in this series converges on the same final request: share an OTP. This isn't a coincidence. It's because the OTP is the actual lock, and everything before it is just getting you to the door.

What an OTP is actually for

A one-time password exists specifically to confirm that the person completing a transaction or login is the same person who owns the account — a second factor beyond a password or PIN. It's sent directly to you because you're meant to be the only one who sees it.

Why sharing it is almost always the point of no return

Once an OTP is shared, whatever action it was generated for — a login, a UPI payment, a card transaction, an account recovery — goes through, and there's typically no undo button on the other side. This is different from, say, sharing your name or even your Aadhaar number, which alone usually isn't enough to complete a financial transaction. An OTP is often the single missing piece a scammer needs.

The legitimate scenarios, and why they don't require sharing

  • Your bank sends an OTP to confirm a transaction — it's meant to be entered by you, into the bank's own app or website, never read aloud to a caller or typed into a message.
  • A delivery service sends an OTP to confirm receipt of a parcel at your door — again, entered by you directly, never shared over a call.
  • No government agency, bank, telecom provider, or delivery service has a legitimate process that requires you to read an OTP to someone else.

The specific phrasing to watch for

  • "Read out the OTP you just received to verify your identity."
  • "An OTP was sent to your number by mistake — please forward it."
  • "Enter this OTP on the link we sent to complete your KYC/refund/verification."

Every version of this request, regardless of who's asking or how the conversation started, means the same thing: stop.

If you've already shared one

Check your bank account and any linked apps immediately for unauthorized activity. If a transaction has already gone through, call 1930 right away — see The First Hour After Sending Money to a Scammer for exactly what to do next. Change your banking password and PIN from a secure device regardless of whether money moved, since the same access could be attempted again.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is there ever a legitimate reason to share an OTP with someone else?

No — an OTP is designed to be entered only by the account holder, directly into the relevant app or website. No bank, government agency, or delivery service has a legitimate process requiring you to read it to someone else.

What's the difference between sharing an OTP and sharing my Aadhaar number?

An OTP alone is often sufficient to complete a financial transaction or account action immediately and irreversibly, while an Aadhaar number by itself typically isn't enough on its own to move money — which is why OTP requests are treated as the more urgent red flag.

Why do so many different scam scripts all end with an OTP request?

Because the OTP is the actual security barrier — everything earlier in a scam script (urgency, fake credibility, a plausible reason) exists specifically to get a victim to that final, decisive request.

What should I do immediately if I've already shared an OTP?

Check your bank account and linked apps for unauthorized activity right away, and call 1930 immediately if any transaction has already gone through.

Does it matter if the caller claims to be from my own bank?

No — legitimate bank representatives never ask you to read an OTP aloud over a call, regardless of how official the call sounds or what caller ID is displayed.
