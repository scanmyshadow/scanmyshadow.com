The Land Purchase Story That Didn't Add Up: How a Bank Manager Stopped an ₹84 Lakh Scam

Scan My ShadowDecember 4, 20254 min read

TL;DR: An elderly couple near Kinnigoli, Karnataka were told they were under "digital arrest" over a fabricated ₹6 crore money-laundering accusation. When they tried to withdraw ₹84 lakh from their fixed deposit, their story about buying land in Bengaluru didn't hold up under a bank manager's questioning. He alerted Mulki police, who reached the couple's home just in time to stop the transfer.

Most digital arrest interventions in the news involve a family member or the police spotting the scam directly. This one turned on something quieter — a bank manager noticing that a cover story didn't quite fit, and refusing to let urgency override his own judgment.

What happened in Kinnigoli

On December 1, fraudsters called 84-year-old Benedict Fernandes and his 71-year-old wife Lilly Fernandes at their home in Damas Katte, near Kinnigoli in coastal Karnataka. The callers claimed to be from Uttar Pradesh CID and accused the couple of receiving ₹6 crore in a Mumbai bank account tied to a financial crime. To back up the threat, the scammers shared forged Aadhaar documents and told the couple they were under "digital arrest" — warning them not to leave the house, and threatening that officers would "shoot them" if they tried.

Terrified, the couple went to their bank branch and told the manager, identified as Royston, that they needed to urgently transfer ₹84 lakh from their fixed deposit to their savings account. When he asked why, they said they were buying land in Bengaluru.

The explanation didn't sit right. The next morning, when the couple returned insisting again on the transfer, their persistence raised further suspicion. Royston alerted the local beat officer and station intelligence personnel. When police visited the couple's home, the actual situation — the fabricated arrest, the threats, the pressure — came out, and officers stepped in to stop the transfer before any money moved.

Why the cover story is the detail worth noticing

Fraudsters running digital arrest scams don't just threaten victims — they often coach them on what to say if a bank asks questions, precisely because large, unusual withdrawals tend to draw scrutiny. That's exactly what happened here: the land-purchase explanation was almost certainly scripted by the scammers to get past exactly this kind of questioning. It worked in the sense that it gave a plausible-sounding reason. It failed because the manager didn't just accept the reason — he noticed the mismatch between the couple's visible fear and the calm story they were offering, and trusted that instinct enough to escalate it.

What this means if you work at a bank, or have elderly parents who do their banking in person

  • Large, unusual withdrawal requests from elderly customers are worth a second question — not because every one is fraud, but because the pattern is common enough to warrant it.
  • A rehearsed-sounding explanation under visible distress is itself a signal. Genuine urgency and a calm, ready cover story don't usually go together.
  • Escalating to police doesn't require certainty — Royston didn't need proof it was a scam, just enough suspicion to involve someone who could ask more questions.

Frequently Asked Questions

Did the couple lose any money in this case?

No. The bank manager's suspicion and the police intervention stopped the ₹84 lakh transfer before it was completed.

What made the bank manager suspicious?

The couple's urgent, repeated insistence on a large transfer combined with a land-purchase explanation that didn't match their visible distress prompted him to alert local police.

Is it normal for digital arrest scammers to coach victims on what to tell a bank?

Yes, it's a documented pattern — scammers often prepare victims with a cover story specifically to get past bank scrutiny on large or unusual withdrawals.

What should I do if a bank employee questions my withdrawal and I'm actually being scammed?

Tell them the truth. Bank staff who ask questions about large withdrawals are often your best chance to break out of a scam call safely.
