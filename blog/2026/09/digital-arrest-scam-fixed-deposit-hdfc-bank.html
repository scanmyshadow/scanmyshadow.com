The Video Call That Gave Away a ₹1.12 Crore Digital Arrest Scam

Scan My Shadow · September 2, 2026

TL;DR: An elderly couple walked into an HDFC Bank branch in Rohini, Delhi to liquidate ₹1.12 crore in fixed deposits and send it to an account they'd never used before. A staff member noticed they were anxious — and that they were on a video call with someone the whole time. That was enough to stop it.

Some of the clearest signs of a digital arrest scam aren't in what a customer says. They're in what's happening on the screen in their hand while they say it.

What happened in Rohini

HDFC Bank, India's largest private sector lender, said its staff at a branch in New Delhi's Rohini helped prevent a ₹1.12 crore fraud on a senior citizen. An elderly couple came in to liquidate fixed deposits worth ₹1.12 crore and requested the funds be transferred to a third-party account the branch didn't recognize as belonging to them.

What made the request stand out wasn't the amount alone — large withdrawals happen for legitimate reasons all the time. It was that both members of the couple appeared visibly anxious, and were constantly speaking to someone over an active video call while the transaction was being processed. According to HDFC Bank's official statement, staff noticed the combination and engaged directly with the couple before the transfer went through, which is what surfaced the fraud in progress.

Why the video call was the real tell

A person calmly withdrawing their own savings doesn't usually need to stay on a call with someone else the entire time they're doing it. An active video call running throughout a large, unusual transaction is one of the most consistent signatures of a digital arrest scam — it's how fraudsters maintain control and make sure their instructions are followed in real time, right up to the moment the money moves.

That's also exactly why bank staff are trained to notice it. A withdrawal request paired with visible distress and an ongoing call is a pattern that shows up again and again in these cases, and it's often the single clearest signal available to someone standing at a bank counter who has no other context about what's happening.

The pattern behind this scam

  • A large, one-time liquidation. Fixed deposits are attractive to scammers precisely because they represent a big, easily movable sum in a single transaction.
  • An unfamiliar destination account. A transfer request to an account the customer has never used before is a strong red flag on its own.
  • Constant remote supervision. Staying on a call throughout the process is how fraudsters prevent a victim from pausing to think or ask someone else.
  • Visible fear over confidence. Someone making a routine, planned withdrawal doesn't usually look distressed doing it.

What to do if you're the one at the counter

  • End any active call before completing a large transaction — a legitimate withdrawal doesn't require someone else's real-time supervision.
  • Double-check the destination account against ones you actually recognize and have used before.
  • If a bank employee asks you to step aside and talk, take the offer seriously. That's exactly the pause that worked here.
  • Report suspected fraud to 1930 or cybercrime.gov.in as early as possible, even if you're not fully certain yet.

Scammers doing this kind of targeting often already have enough personal detail — a name, an address, sometimes account information — to make their story sound credible from the first call. Frequently Asked Questions

What tipped off the bank staff?

Two things together: the couple's visible anxiety, and the fact that they were on an active video call with someone while trying to move a large sum to an account they didn't recognize as their own.

Why do scammers target fixed deposits specifically?

Fixed deposits represent large, consolidated sums that are simple to liquidate in one transaction, unlike smaller recurring accounts. That combination of size and liquidity makes them a preferred target.

Is it normal for bank staff to question a withdrawal request?

Yes. Banks increasingly train frontline staff to treat unusual withdrawal patterns, visible distress, or an active phone or video call as reasons to pause and ask questions before processing a large transfer.
