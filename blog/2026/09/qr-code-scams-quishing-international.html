QR Code Scams ("Quishing") Internationally: Parking Meters, Menus, and More

Scan My ShadowSeptember 3, 20263 min read

TL;DR: "Quishing" — QR code phishing — covers two related risks: fraudulent stickers placed over legitimate codes at parking meters or menus that redirect your payment, and QR codes in emails or flyers that lead to phishing sites. Both are increasingly common internationally.

QR codes became a normal part of daily payments and menus quickly enough that verification habits haven't caught up — which is exactly the gap this scam exploits.

Physical QR code tampering

Scammers print a fraudulent QR code sticker and place it directly over a legitimate one at a parking meter, restaurant table, or payment kiosk. Scanning it takes you to a fake payment page designed to capture your card details, or redirects a genuine payment to the scammer's account instead of the business or municipality.

  • Before scanning at a physical location, check whether the sticker looks layered or slightly raised compared to the surrounding surface — a sign it's been placed over another code.
  • After scanning, check that the URL and business name shown match what you'd expect before entering any payment information.
  • If a parking payment app or official municipal option is available, use it directly rather than a QR code when possible.

QR codes in emails, texts, and flyers

Because QR codes hide the actual destination URL until scanned, they're increasingly used in phishing emails and texts specifically to bypass link-scanning security tools that check visible URLs. A message might ask you to scan a code to "verify your account," "claim a reward," or "reschedule a delivery."

  • Treat a QR code in an unsolicited email or text with the same skepticism as a suspicious link — because functionally, it is one.
  • Most phones show a preview of the destination URL before opening it after scanning — check this before tapping through, and don't proceed if it doesn't match the sender's claimed identity.

The pattern to remember

A QR code is just a link in a different format — every instinct you'd apply to a suspicious link (checking the URL, verifying the sender, avoiding entering payment details on unfamiliar sites) applies equally here.

Frequently Asked Questions

How can I tell if a QR code sticker has been tampered with?

Check whether it looks layered or slightly raised compared to the surrounding surface, which can indicate a fraudulent sticker has been placed over a legitimate code.

Why do scammers use QR codes in phishing emails instead of regular links?

QR codes hide the actual destination URL until scanned, which can help them bypass automated link-scanning security tools that check visible URLs in emails and texts.

Is it safe to scan a QR code before checking where it leads?

Most phones show a preview of the destination URL before fully opening it — check this preview and confirm it matches what you'd expect before proceeding.

What should I do if I paid through a QR code and suspect it was fraudulent?

Contact your card issuer or bank immediately to flag the account and monitor closely for unauthorized charges.

Is there a safer alternative to scanning a QR code for parking payment?

Use the municipality's or garage's official app or website directly when available, rather than a QR code posted in a public location where tampering is possible.
