Who Called Me? Free Reverse Phone Lookup Options in the US, Ranked by Honesty

By Siddharth Shankar · November 2, 2026
Phone LookupCaller IDUnited StatesHow To

TL;DR: Free reverse phone lookups can sometimes name a business or show spam reports, but for personal mobiles most "free" sites give a teaser (city, carrier) and push a paid report, often with outdated data. And if the call was spoofed, any lookup points to an innocent person. The most reliable free steps: search the number in quotes, check your carrier’s spam label, watch for the "verified" caller ID mark, let unknown calls go to voicemail, and never call back a number that pressures you. Unwanted-call complaints to the FTC are still about 48% lower than in 2021, but imposters remain one of the most reported call types.

Why everyone is looking up numbers

A missed call from an unknown number now comes with a question attached: is this my doctor, a delivery driver or a scammer? The FTC’s 2025 Do Not Call data shows why people are wary:

  • robocalls "continued to make up most" Do Not Call complaints in fiscal year 2025;
  • calls about reducing debt, imposters, and medical and prescription issues were among the most reported;
  • unwanted-call reports are still about 48% lower than in FY2021, when the FTC received roughly five million reports, but complaints rose again in 2025;
  • the Do Not Call Registry had about 258.5 million active registrations by 30 September 2025.

What "verified" caller ID means

US phone companies use STIR/SHAKEN (also written SHAKEN/STIR), a framework the FCC pushed the industry to adopt, where the carrier that starts a call digitally "signs" the caller ID and other carriers validate it before the call reaches you. The FCC says the aim is to counter illegal spoofing, help unmask spoofed calls and give consumers information showing which calls are authenticated.

On many phones that shows up as a checkmark or "Verified." It’s useful, but read it narrowly: it confirms the number probably wasn’t faked. It doesn’t tell you whether the person on the line is telling the truth.

The spoofing problem

Scammers routinely fake caller ID. The FCC describes "neighbor spoofing," where a number looks local or similar to yours to make you pick up. Look up a spoofed number and you’ll find an ordinary person who has no idea their number was used. Please don’t ring them up angry.

That’s also why no lookup can prove a call from "your bank" or "the IRS" is real. Only calling the official number yourself can. See how spoofing works.

Why the wrong person comes up

  • Spoofing: the number was faked.
  • Recycled numbers: carriers reassign disconnected numbers, so old owners linger in databases.
  • Stale broker data: people-search sites mix records from many years and sources.
  • VoIP numbers: internet numbers can be created and dropped in minutes, leaving little trail.

A safer routine for unknown calls

  1. Let it go to voicemail. Real callers usually leave a message.
  2. Search the number in quotes, plus words like "scam" or "complaint."
  3. If it claims to be a company or agency, hang up and call the number on your statement, card or the official website.
  4. Don’t press buttons to "stop calls" and avoid yes-or-no questions. The FCC’s robocall guide warns against both.
  5. Turn on your carrier’s free spam filter in its app or account settings.
  6. Report scam calls to the FTC and add your number to the Do Not Call Registry. The registry won’t stop criminals, but it makes it easier to spot illegal callers.

When a lookup is genuinely worth it

  • A business number that keeps calling: searching usually names the company.
  • A missed call before an appointment: check against the provider’s website.
  • A text with a link: don’t look up the number, just don’t tap the link. See smishing.

The flip side: your number is searchable too

If anyone can type a number and get a name and address, so can anyone with yours. Remove your listings from USPhonebook, TruePeopleSearch and Whitepages, and read how to remove your number from the internet. Google’s Results about you tool can also flag search results showing your phone number.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is there a truly free reverse phone lookup?

Searching the number in quotes and your carrier’s spam labels are free. Many "free" lookup sites show a teaser and charge for the details.

Why does a reverse lookup show the wrong person?

The number may have been spoofed, reassigned to a new owner, or the site’s data may simply be out of date.

What does the verified checkmark on caller ID mean?

The call passed STIR/SHAKEN authentication, so the number probably wasn’t spoofed. It doesn’t mean the caller is honest.

Can I find out who owns a mobile number?

Sometimes, through people-search sites, but results are often outdated and those same sites list your information too.

Should I call back an unknown number?

Not if it pressured you or claimed to be a bank or agency. Call the official number instead.

Where do I report scam calls in the US?

Report them to the FTC at ReportFraud.ftc.gov.
