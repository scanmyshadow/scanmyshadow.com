Scam and Data Privacy Glossary: 66 Terms in Plain English

By Siddharth Shankar · November 24, 2026
GlossaryScam PreventionData Privacy

TL;DR: This glossary explains 66 scam, fraud and data privacy terms in plain English. Each entry says what the term means, how the trick works, the warning signs and what to do. Almost every scam here relies on the same three things: urgency, a trusted name, and a request for a code, a payment or remote access.

Phishing and impersonation

Messages, calls and fake sites that pretend to be someone you trust.

Smishing (SMS Phishing) Vishing (Voice Phishing) Spear Phishing Whaling Attacks Clone Phishing Angler Phishing Quishing (QR Code Phishing) Business Email Compromise Pretexting Caller ID Spoofing Wardialing and Robocalls Typosquatting

Smishing (SMS Phishing)

Smishing is phishing carried out over SMS text message instead of email, using fake links or urgent requests to steal personal information or money.

Full guide: What Is Smishing? How It Works and How to Protect Yourself

Vishing (Voice Phishing)

Vishing (voice phishing) is a scam carried out over a phone call, where a caller impersonates a trusted organization to extract personal information or money.

In India: A caller claims to be from your bank, RBI, or a payment app's support team, citing a fake issue like a blocked account, unauthorized transaction, or KYC problem. Using caller ID spoofing to appear legitimate, they create urgency and ask the victim to share their card number, CVV, OTP, or to install a remote-access app to "fix" the issue. Banks and RBI never call asking for your OTP, PIN, or CVV. Hang up and call your bank back using the number on your card or their official website, never a number the caller gives you.

Full guide: What Is Vishing? How It Works and How to Protect Yourself

Spear Phishing

Spear phishing is a targeted phishing attack aimed at a specific individual, using personal details to make the message far more convincing than a generic scam email.

How it works: Unlike mass phishing, an attacker researches a specific target — using information from social media, previous breaches, or public records — to craft a message that references real details, making it much harder to spot as fake.

Warning signs: An email that references accurate personal or work details you wouldn't expect a stranger to know, combined with an unusual request or urgency.

Protect yourself: Be cautious even with messages that seem to know real details about you, verify unusual requests through a separate channel, and limit how much personal information is publicly available.

Whaling Attacks

Whaling is a highly targeted phishing attack aimed specifically at senior executives, using personalized, well-researched messages to steal credentials or trigger fraudulent transactions.

How it works: Attackers research a specific executive using public information (LinkedIn, press releases, company filings) to craft a convincing, personalized email — often impersonating a board member, lawyer, or regulator — requesting an urgent wire transfer or sensitive data.

Warning signs: An unusually personalized email referencing real details, urgency around a confidential or time-sensitive matter, and a request that bypasses normal approval processes.

Protect yourself: Verify unusual or urgent requests through a separate channel, limit how much personal and organizational detail is publicly available, and require multi-person approval for large transfers.

Clone Phishing

Clone phishing is when an attacker copies a legitimate email you've already received and resends it with malicious links or attachments swapped in.

How it works: The attacker takes a real, previously delivered email (often one with an attachment or link you'd trust) and sends an almost identical copy, claiming it's a resend or update, with the original link or attachment replaced by a malicious one.

Warning signs: An email that looks like one you've seen before but arrives again unexpectedly, slight differences in the sender's address, and a link that doesn't match what you'd expect from the original.

Protect yourself: Check the sender's actual email address carefully, hover over links before clicking to see the real destination, and verify with the supposed sender through another channel if anything feels off.

Angler Phishing

Angler phishing is when scammers impersonate a company's customer support on social media to intercept and exploit customers reaching out for help.

How it works: A scammer creates a fake account that looks like a company's official support handle, then monitors social media for people complaining or asking that company for help, and reaches out first with a link or request for account details before the real company can respond.

Warning signs: A support account with a slightly altered handle or few followers, a DM asking for personal or account information, and a link to a site that doesn't match the company's real domain.

Protect yourself: Only trust verified/official accounts, never share account or payment details over social media DMs, and go directly to the company's official app or website for support.

Quishing (QR Code Phishing)

Quishing is phishing carried out through a malicious QR code that, when scanned, leads to a fake website designed to steal information or install malware.

How it works: A scammer places a fake QR code sticker over a legitimate one (on a parking meter, restaurant menu, or flyer) or sends one via email or text. Scanning it takes you to a convincing fake login page or triggers a malicious download.

Warning signs: A QR code that looks like it's been stuck over another one, a QR code from an unsolicited email or text, and a resulting webpage that asks for login or payment details unexpectedly.

Protect yourself: Preview the URL before opening it if your phone shows one, avoid scanning QR codes from unsolicited sources, and never enter login or payment details on a page you reached via QR code without verifying the site first.

Deeper guide: QR Code Scams (Quishing) Internationally | Scan My Shadow

Business Email Compromise

Business email compromise (BEC) is a scam where an attacker impersonates a company executive or vendor via email to trick an employee into wiring money or sensitive data.

How it works: Attackers often compromise or spoof a real executive's or vendor's email account, then send an urgent request to an employee in finance to wire funds or change payment details for an upcoming invoice — timed to catch people off guard.

Warning signs: An unusual request from a senior executive, urgency to bypass normal approval processes, last-minute changes to payment or bank details, and slight misspellings in the sender's email domain.

Protect yourself: Verify any payment or bank detail change by phone using a number you already have on file — never one provided in the email — and enforce a policy requiring verbal confirmation for wire transfers.

Pretexting

Pretexting is a social engineering technique where a scammer invents a false scenario (a pretext) to convince someone to hand over information or access they normally wouldn't.

How it works: A scammer might pose as IT support, a new coworker, a survey taker, or a delivery driver — building a believable story to get you to reveal a password, click a link, or let them into a building or system.

Warning signs: An unsolicited request that requires you to "verify" information you'd normally never share, a scenario that creates urgency or plays on helpfulness, and small inconsistencies in the story if you ask follow-up questions.

Protect yourself: Verify any unexpected request through a separate, known channel before acting on it — call the company back, confirm with a colleague, or check with the actual department involved.

Caller ID Spoofing

Caller ID spoofing is when a scammer fakes the phone number that appears on your caller ID, making a call look like it's coming from a trusted source such as your bank or a local number.

How it works: Using widely available spoofing software, a scammer can display any number they choose — often your bank's real customer service line, a government agency, or even a number similar to your own. This makes vishing (voice phishing) calls far more convincing.

Warning signs: A call from a number that matches a real organization but with an unusual tone, urgency, or request that the real organization wouldn't make, or a call from what looks like your own number.

Protect yourself: Never trust caller ID alone to verify who's calling. Hang up and call the organization back using a number from their official website.

Wardialing and Robocalls

Wardialing is an automated technique that dials large blocks of phone numbers in sequence to find active lines, often used to deliver scam robocalls at scale.

How it works: Scammers use auto-dialers to call thousands of numbers, often filtering for which ones are active and answered, so future scam calls can be targeted more efficiently — sometimes with an automated message designed to get you to press a button or stay on the line.

Warning signs: A robocall with no live person, a recorded message creating urgency, and a prompt to press a number or stay on the line, which often confirms your number is active for future targeting.

Protect yourself: Hang up immediately without pressing any buttons, don't engage even to say "stop calling," and register your number with your country's do-not-call registry.

Typosquatting

Typosquatting is when a scammer registers a domain name that's a common misspelling of a popular website to trick visitors who mistype the real address.

How it works: A scammer buys a domain like "amaz0n.com" or "paypa1.com" and builds a convincing fake login page. Anyone who mistypes the real address, or clicks a link using the fake domain, may unknowingly enter their credentials on the fake site.

Warning signs: A URL that's almost but not quite right, unexpected redirects, and a login page that looks correct but the address bar doesn't match the real company's domain.

Protect yourself: Double-check the URL before entering login details, use bookmarks for sites you visit often instead of retyping the address, and enable browser warnings for suspicious sites.

Account and identity takeover

How criminals get into your accounts, or build a fake identity from your data.

Account Takeover Fraud SIM Swapping (SIM Swap Fraud) OTP Fraud KYC Update Fraud Session Hijacking Man-in-the-Middle Attacks Man-in-the-Browser Attacks Synthetic Identity Fraud Screen-Sharing App Fraud

Account Takeover Fraud

Account takeover fraud is when a scammer gains unauthorized access to your existing online account — often using stolen or leaked credentials — and uses it for fraud.

How it works: Attackers typically use credentials leaked in a previous data breach (credential stuffing), phishing, or SIM swapping to log into your account, then change the password or recovery details to lock you out while they drain funds or misuse the account.

Warning signs: Login alerts from unfamiliar locations or devices, being logged out unexpectedly, changed account details you didn't update, and activity you don't recognize.

Protect yourself: Use a unique password for every account, enable two-factor authentication, and check whether your credentials have already appeared in a known data breach.

SIM Swapping (SIM Swap Fraud)

SIM swapping is when a scammer convinces your mobile carrier to transfer your phone number to a SIM card they control, letting them intercept your calls and SMS-based verification codes.

How it works: The scammer gathers enough personal information about you (often from a data breach or social engineering) to convince your carrier's support team they are you, then requests your number be moved to a new SIM. Once successful, they can receive your two-factor authentication codes and take over bank, email, and crypto accounts.

In India: Scammers first gather personal details through phishing, data leaks, or social engineering, then either trick the victim into calling a fake customer care number and pressing a code that reroutes calls, or directly contact the telecom operator's outlet with forged documents to request a duplicate SIM. Once the swap goes through, the victim's original SIM stops working and the fraudster starts receiving all OTPs and bank alerts.

Warning signs: Your phone suddenly loses signal or shows "No Service" unexpectedly, you receive an alert about a SIM change you didn't request, or you're locked out of accounts that use SMS verification.

Protect yourself: Set up a PIN or passcode on your mobile account with your carrier, use an authenticator app instead of SMS for two-factor authentication where possible, and contact your carrier immediately if you lose signal unexpectedly.

Deeper guide: SIM Swap Fraud: How It Works and How to Protect Your Number

OTP Fraud

OTP fraud is when a scammer tricks a victim into sharing the one-time password sent to their phone, using it to authorize a transaction, login, or SIM/card action on the victim's account.

How it works: A caller posing as bank staff, a delivery agent, or a lottery representative claims an OTP is needed to "verify," "cancel," or "confirm" something, when in reality entering that OTP approves a transaction the scammer has already initiated — such as a UPI transfer, card payment, or account change.

Warning signs: Any unsolicited call or message asking you to read out or share an OTP, an OTP SMS you receive that you didn't request, and pressure to share it quickly "before it expires."

Protect yourself: Never share an OTP with anyone over phone, SMS, or WhatsApp, even if they claim to be from your bank. Banks and legitimate services never call asking for your OTP.

KYC Update Fraud

KYC update fraud is when scammers send fake "update your KYC or your account will be blocked" messages or calls, directing victims to phishing links or fraudulent apps that steal their banking credentials.

How it works: A message or call, often impersonating a bank, wallet provider, or LPG/electricity company, warns that KYC is incomplete and the account or connection will be suspended within hours unless updated via a provided link. The link leads to a fake page or prompts installation of a screen-sharing app, through which credentials or OTPs are stolen.

Warning signs: Urgency about your account being blocked within hours, a link that doesn't match your bank or provider's official domain, and any request to install a remote-access app to "complete" KYC.

Protect yourself: Never update KYC through a link sent via SMS or WhatsApp. Go directly to your bank's app, official website, or nearest branch to update KYC, and never share OTPs with anyone.

Deeper guide: KYC Update Scam Calls | Scan My Shadow

Session Hijacking

Session hijacking is when an attacker steals an active login session (rather than your password) to gain access to your account without needing to log in themselves.

How it works: Attackers often steal a session cookie through malware, an unsecured network, or a phishing kit that intercepts login tokens in real time — bypassing even two-factor authentication since the session is already authenticated.

Warning signs: Being logged out unexpectedly, seeing account activity from an unfamiliar location while you were still logged in, and unexpected changes to account settings.

Protect yourself: Log out of accounts on shared or public devices, avoid entering credentials on unsecured WiFi, and regularly review and revoke active sessions in your account's security settings.

Man-in-the-Middle Attacks

A man-in-the-middle attack is when an attacker secretly intercepts communication between two parties — often over unsecured WiFi — to eavesdrop or alter the data being sent.

How it works: On an unsecured public WiFi network, an attacker can position themselves between your device and the internet, capturing unencrypted data like login credentials, or redirecting you to fake versions of real websites.

Warning signs: A website warning about an invalid security certificate, unexpectedly being logged out of accounts on public WiFi, and a network name that looks slightly off or duplicated.

Protect yourself: Avoid entering sensitive information on public WiFi, use a VPN when on networks you don't control, and make sure websites you use show "https" with a valid certificate.

Man-in-the-Browser Attacks

A man-in-the-browser attack uses malware installed on your device to secretly alter what you see and enter in your browser — even on legitimate, secure websites.

How it works: Malware embeds itself in your browser and can modify a real banking site's page in real time — for example, changing a transfer's destination account after you've entered and confirmed it, without any visible sign anything is wrong.

Warning signs: This is very hard to detect visually since the site itself looks completely normal. Unexplained transactions despite using only legitimate, secure sites are the main warning sign.

Protect yourself: Keep your operating system, browser, and antivirus software up to date, avoid downloading software from untrusted sources, and monitor your accounts closely for unauthorized transactions.

Synthetic Identity Fraud

Synthetic identity fraud is when a scammer combines real information (like a stolen Social Security number) with fabricated details to create an entirely new, fake identity used to open accounts and build credit.

How it works: A scammer often uses a real SSN belonging to someone unlikely to check their credit regularly (a child, an elderly person, or someone deceased) combined with a made-up name and birthdate, then slowly builds credit history before maxing out loans and disappearing.

Warning signs: Unexplained credit inquiries under a name and details that don't quite match yours, and credit monitoring alerts for accounts you don't recognize tied to your SSN.

Protect yourself: Regularly check your credit report for unfamiliar accounts, freeze your child's credit file if you haven't already, and consider credit monitoring that flags new-account activity.

Screen-Sharing App Fraud

Screen-sharing app fraud is when a scammer, posing as bank or customer support, convinces a victim to install a remote-access app like AnyDesk or TeamViewer to "help" resolve an issue, then uses that access to see the victim's screen and drain their bank or UPI accounts.

Card and payment fraud

Tricks that target your cards, bank transfers and payment apps.

UPI Fraud Card Skimming Skimming vs Shimming Formjacking Juice Jacking Wire Fraud Fake Check Scams Money Mule Scams Brushing Scams Subscription Trap Scams Wardriving

UPI Fraud

UPI fraud covers scams that exploit India's Unified Payments Interface — including fake payment request links, malicious QR codes, and "collect request" tricks — to get victims to unknowingly approve a payment out of their account instead of receiving one.

How it works: A scammer sends a UPI "collect request" disguised as a refund, cashback, or payment received notification, or shares a QR code claiming scanning it will credit money. In reality, approving the request or scanning the code authorizes a payment out of the victim's account, entering their UPI PIN completes the transfer to the scammer.

Warning signs: Any request to enter your UPI PIN to "receive" money (receiving money never requires a PIN), an unexpected collect request or QR code from an unknown seller or "buyer", and urgency to approve a payment quickly.

Protect yourself: Never enter your UPI PIN to receive a payment — a PIN is only needed to send money. Verify collect requests carefully before approving, and only scan QR codes from sources you trust.

Deeper guide: UPI Fraud Prevention India: Complete Guide for 2026

Card Skimming

Card skimming is when a scammer installs a hidden device on an ATM, gas pump, or payment terminal to steal your card's information when you use it.

How it works: A skimmer device, often disguised to look like part of the machine, reads and stores your card's magnetic stripe data as you swipe. Some are paired with a hidden camera or fake keypad overlay to capture your PIN as well.

Warning signs: A card reader that looks loose, bulky, or different in color from the rest of the machine, a keypad that feels unusually thick, and resistance when inserting your card.

Protect yourself: Use ATMs at bank branches rather than standalone locations when possible, cover the keypad when entering your PIN, and check your statements regularly for unfamiliar charges.

Skimming vs Shimming

Skimming steals your card's magnetic stripe data using a device attached to a card reader; shimming does the same for chip cards using a thin device inserted into the card slot itself.

How it works: A skimmer sits on top of or over a real card reader to copy stripe data as you swipe. A shimmer is a much thinner device inserted directly into the slot, designed to read chip card data — both are usually paired with a hidden camera to capture your PIN.

Warning signs: A card reader that feels loose or looks different from the rest of the machine (skimming), or unusual resistance when inserting a chip card (shimming).

Protect yourself: Use ATMs at bank branches, cover the keypad when entering your PIN, and check your statements regularly for unfamiliar transactions.

Formjacking

Formjacking is when attackers inject malicious code into a legitimate website's checkout or form page to secretly capture the data entered — often credit card details.

How it works: Attackers compromise a website's code (often through a vulnerable third-party script) to intercept form submissions in real time, sending the data to themselves while the transaction still appears to go through normally.

Warning signs: This is nearly invisible to shoppers — the checkout process looks completely normal. The main warning sign is unexplained fraudulent charges appearing after shopping on a particular site.

Protect yourself: Use a credit card rather than a debit card for online shopping (better fraud protection), monitor your statements regularly, and consider a virtual card number for online purchases where available.

Juice Jacking

Juice jacking is when a compromised public USB charging port or cable is used to install malware or steal data from your device while it charges.

How it works: A public charging station (at an airport, cafe, or hotel) is compromised or fitted with a malicious cable that, when connected, can access your device's data or install malware — exploiting the fact that USB connections carry both power and data.

Warning signs: This is difficult to detect visually. The safest assumption is to treat any public charging port or unfamiliar cable as a potential risk.

Protect yourself: Use your own charging cable and a wall outlet instead of a public USB port where possible, or use a "USB data blocker" adapter that allows charging but blocks data transfer.

Deeper guide: Is Your Charging Cable Compromised? Juice Jacking Check

Wire Fraud

Wire fraud broadly refers to any scam that convinces a victim to send money via wire transfer, which is difficult to reverse once completed — including business email compromise and romance scams.

How it works: Wire fraud often combines with another scam technique (a fake invoice, a romance scam, a real estate closing scam) to convince the victim the transfer is legitimate and urgent, exploiting the fact that wire transfers are hard to reverse once sent.

Warning signs: Urgency, last-minute changes to payment instructions, and pressure to use wire transfer specifically rather than a more traceable or reversible payment method.

Protect yourself: Verify any wire transfer instructions by phone using a number you already know is correct, especially for large transactions like real estate closings, before sending.

Deeper guide: Wire Recall & Wire Fraud Recovery in the US | Scan My Shadow

Fake Check Scams

A fake check scam involves a scammer sending a victim a fraudulent check, asking them to deposit it and wire back a portion, before the check bounces days later.

How it works: The scammer overpays for something (a purchase, a job, a prize) with a check for more than the amount owed, then asks the victim to wire back the difference. The check takes days to be flagged as fake, by which point the wired money is gone.

Warning signs: Being asked to deposit a check and immediately wire part of it back, an overpayment with an excuse, and pressure to act before you'd normally verify the check has cleared.

Protect yourself: Never wire money based on funds from a check that hasn't fully cleared — banks are required to make funds available quickly, but that doesn't mean the check is legitimate.

Deeper guide: How to Spot a Fake Check Before You Deposit It

Money Mule Scams

A money mule scam recruits people (often unknowingly) to receive and forward stolen money, making them an unwitting link in the laundering chain — and potentially criminally liable.

How it works: Scammers recruit victims through fake job offers ("payment processing agent") or romance scams, asking them to receive money into their own bank account and forward it elsewhere, taking a cut. The money is usually stolen from other scam victims.

Warning signs: A "job" that involves receiving and forwarding money through your own account, a too-good-to-be-true payment for minimal work, and vague explanations about the company's actual business.

Protect yourself: Never agree to receive and forward money through your own bank account for someone else, even for a job offer — this can make you criminally liable even if you didn't know the money was stolen.

Brushing Scams

A brushing scam is when a seller ships you unordered items (often cheap goods) using your address without your knowledge, then posts a fake verified review under your name to boost their product's ratings.

How it works: Scammers obtain your name and address (often from a data breach or public listing) and use it to place a fake order on a marketplace, paying for it themselves. Once "delivered," they can post a verified purchase review to make their listing look more credible.

Warning signs: Unordered packages arriving at your address, packages addressed to you from sellers you've never heard of, and unexplained charges you don't recognize (rare, but worth checking).

Protect yourself: Check your accounts on the marketplace the package came from for orders you didn't place, report it to the platform, and check whether your name and address may be exposed elsewhere online.

Subscription Trap Scams

A subscription trap is when a 'free trial' or one-time purchase quietly enrolls you in a recurring subscription that's deliberately difficult to cancel.

How it works: A tempting free trial or low one-time price requires your card details upfront, with recurring charges beginning automatically after the trial — often with a confusing or deliberately hidden cancellation process designed to keep you subscribed.

Warning signs: A free trial or cheap offer that requires full card details, unclear terms about when charges begin, and a cancellation process that's hard to find or requires calling during limited hours.

Protect yourself: Read the fine print before entering card details for any "free" trial, set a calendar reminder before the trial ends, and monitor your statements for charges you don't recognize.

Wardriving

Wardriving is the act of searching for unsecured WiFi networks, often by driving around with a laptop or phone, to gain unauthorized access to them.

Full guide: What Is Wardriving? How It Works and How to Protect Yourself

Romance, family and charity scams

Scams that work on trust, emotion and goodwill.

Catfishing Pig Butchering Scams Sextortion Scams Grandparent Scams AI Voice Cloning Scams Deepfake Scams Puppy Scams Crowdfunding Scams Fake Charity Scams Lottery and Sweepstakes Scams Advance Fee Fraud

Catfishing

Catfishing is when someone creates a fake online identity — often with stolen photos — to deceive another person, usually in a romantic context.

How it works: A scammer builds a fake profile using photos taken from someone else's real social media, builds an online relationship, and may avoid video calls or meeting in person while working toward an eventual request for money.

Warning signs: Refusal to video call or meet in person, inconsistencies in their story, and photos that seem too polished (a reverse image search often reveals they're stolen).

Protect yourself: Do a reverse image search on profile photos before investing emotional or financial trust, insist on a video call early, and never send money to someone you haven't met in person.

Pig Butchering Scams

A pig butchering scam is a long-con investment fraud where a scammer builds a fake relationship over weeks or months before convincing the victim to invest in a fraudulent platform, usually crypto.

How it works: Scammers make initial contact (often a "wrong number" text or dating app match), build trust and rapport over time, then introduce a lucrative-seeming investment opportunity on a platform they control. Early small withdrawals build confidence before the victim is convinced to invest much larger sums, which are then unrecoverable.

Warning signs: An unsolicited contact that turns into a fast-building relationship, talk of trading or investment "expertise," a platform you've never heard of showing suspiciously consistent gains, and resistance to video calls or meeting in person.

Protect yourself: Never invest based on a relationship built entirely online, verify any platform is a registered broker before depositing money, and be suspicious of consistently high returns with no risk.

Deeper guide: 'Pig Butchering' Romance-Investment Scams in India

Sextortion Scams

Sextortion is when a scammer, often posing as a stranger on social media or WhatsApp, initiates a video call that is secretly recorded and turns explicit, then blackmails the victim with the recording, demanding money to prevent it from being shared with their contacts.

How it works: The scammer adds the victim on WhatsApp or social media, quickly escalates to a video call that turns intimate, and secretly screen-records it. Immediately after, they threaten to send the recording to the victim's family, friends, or colleagues unless a payment is made — and often continue demanding more money even after being paid.

Warning signs: An unfamiliar contact quickly pushing for a video call, a call that turns explicit unexpectedly, and any subsequent threat to share a recording unless money is paid.

Protect yourself: End the call and do not pay — payment rarely stops further demands. Block the contact, avoid engaging further, and report it immediately to the National Cybercrime Helpline (1930), which has dedicated support for this type of case.

Grandparent Scams

A grandparent scam is when a scammer calls an elderly person pretending to be a grandchild in urgent trouble, asking for money to be sent immediately and kept secret.

How it works: The scammer calls claiming to be a grandchild who's been arrested, in an accident, or stranded abroad, often citing a vague reason for why their voice sounds different, and pressures the victim to wire money or buy gift cards immediately, asking them not to tell other family members.

Warning signs: Urgency, a request for secrecy, a request for payment via gift card or wire transfer, and a vague explanation for why the caller's voice sounds off.

Protect yourself: Hang up and call the grandchild directly on a number you already have, or call another family member to verify before sending any money.

Deeper guide: Grandparent Scams Internationally | Scan My Shadow

AI Voice Cloning Scams

An AI voice cloning scam uses artificial intelligence to recreate a real person's voice — often a family member — convincingly enough to trick victims into sending money urgently.

How it works: Scammers use short audio clips (often pulled from social media) to clone a voice, then call claiming to be that person in an emergency — an accident, arrest, or kidnapping — asking for money to be wired immediately, often asking the victim to keep it secret.

Warning signs: Extreme urgency, a request for secrecy, pressure to send money via wire transfer or gift cards immediately, and a voice that sounds right but the conversation feels oddly scripted or avoids specific personal details.

Protect yourself: Hang up and call the person directly on a number you already have for them. Agree on a family "safe word" in advance for genuine emergencies, and never send money based on a phone call alone.

Deeper guide: AI Voice Cloning Scam Calls | Scan My Shadow

Deepfake Scams

A deepfake scam uses AI-generated fake video or audio of a real person to deceive victims — from fake celebrity endorsements to impersonating a company executive.

How it works: Scammers use AI tools to generate convincing fake video or audio of a public figure or executive, often used in fake investment ads or urgent internal company requests, exploiting the credibility of a familiar face or voice.

Warning signs: A video or call that seems slightly off in timing, lighting, or audio sync, an unusual request wrapped in a familiar face, and urgency that discourages you from verifying independently.

Protect yourself: Verify any unusual financial request through a separate, known channel, and be skeptical of investment opportunities "endorsed" by public figures in ads — legitimate endorsements are rare and heavily regulated.

Deeper guide: Deepfake Fraud Statistics 2026

Puppy Scams

A puppy scam advertises a purebred puppy for sale online, collects payment and shipping fees, and never delivers an actual animal.

How it works: Scammers use stolen photos of real puppies, offer an attractively low price, and after collecting an initial payment, invent additional fees (special crate, insurance, vet costs) before disappearing without ever delivering an animal.

Warning signs: A price well below what that breed normally costs, refusal to do a video call with the actual puppy, and requests for additional fees after the initial payment.

Protect yourself: Insist on a live video call with the puppy and seller before paying anything, never pay via wire transfer or gift card, and try to visit in person if at all possible.

Crowdfunding Scams

A crowdfunding scam is a fake fundraising campaign — often exploiting a real tragedy or cause — that collects donations for a cause that doesn't actually exist or benefit as claimed.

How it works: A scammer creates a convincing campaign, often using real photos of an actual tragedy taken from news coverage, soliciting donations that go directly to them rather than any real cause.

Warning signs: A campaign with no verifiable connection to the person or cause claimed, urgency to donate immediately, and an organizer with little or no online history.

Protect yourself: Verify a campaign's authenticity independently before donating — check if local news or the person's own social media confirms it — and consider donating to established, verified organizations instead.

Fake Charity Scams

A fake charity scam is when scammers solicit donations for a charity that doesn't exist, or impersonate a real charity, keeping the money for themselves.

How it works: Scammers often exploit current events (a disaster, a tragedy in the news) to create urgency, using a name similar to a well-known real charity, and pressuring for an immediate donation, often via gift card or wire transfer.

Warning signs: Pressure to donate immediately, a charity name that sounds similar to but isn't quite a known organization, and requests for payment via gift card, cryptocurrency, or wire transfer.

Protect yourself: Verify a charity's legitimacy through an independent registry before donating, and pay by credit card rather than gift card or wire transfer for better fraud protection.

Deeper guide: How to Verify a Charity Before You Donate

Lottery and Sweepstakes Scams

A lottery or sweepstakes scam tells the victim they've won a prize they never entered for, but must pay a fee or tax upfront to claim it.

How it works: The victim receives a call, email, or letter claiming a major prize win, often for a lottery they never entered. To "release" the funds, they're told to pay a processing fee, tax, or customs charge — which just goes to the scammer, with no prize ever arriving.

Warning signs: Winning a lottery or sweepstakes you never entered, any request to pay money to receive a prize, and pressure to keep the win confidential until funds are "processed."

Protect yourself: Legitimate lotteries never require you to pay money to claim a prize. If you didn't enter, you can't have won.

Deeper guide: KBC Lottery WhatsApp Scam: Why the ₹25 Lakh Prize Is Fake

Advance Fee Fraud

Advance fee fraud is when a scammer promises a large future payout (a loan, prize, or inheritance) in exchange for a smaller upfront fee, then disappears once the fee is paid.

How it works: The scammer presents a compelling reason for a large sum of money — a lottery win, loan approval, or inheritance — but claims a fee, tax, or "processing cost" must be paid first. Once paid, the promised payout never arrives.

Warning signs: Any promise of a payout that requires you to pay money first, communication that feels urgent or overly formal, and a payout that's contingent on secrecy or a fee that keeps growing.

Protect yourself: No legitimate lottery, loan, or inheritance requires you to pay a fee upfront before receiving funds. Treat any request like this as a scam.

Digital Arrest Scams

A digital arrest scam is when fraudsters impersonate police, CBI, customs, or narcotics officials on a video or phone call, falsely claiming the victim is linked to an illegal parcel or a criminal case, and pressure them to pay money or stay on camera under "digital house arrest" to avoid arrest.

How it works: The scam typically starts with a call or message claiming a courier addressed to the victim contains drugs, fake passports, or other illegal items. The caller, often in a fake uniform on a video call staged to look like a police station, says the victim is under investigation and must not disconnect or contact anyone. Victims are told to transfer money to a "RBI verification account" or stay visible on camera for hours while their bank accounts are drained.

Warning signs: A call claiming a parcel in your name was intercepted with illegal contents, pressure to stay on video call continuously and not hang up or tell family, threats of immediate arrest, and any request to transfer money to "verify" your identity or clear your name.

Protect yourself: Real police, CBI, or customs officials never conduct investigations, arrests, or "verifications" over a video call, and never ask for money to close a case. Hang up immediately, do not share any personal or banking details, and report the call to the National Cybercrime Helpline (1930).

Deeper guide: Digital Arrest Scams Surge in India | Scan My Shadow

Courier and Parcel Scam Calls

A courier or parcel scam call is when fraudsters posing as customs, courier company, or police officials claim a parcel addressed to the victim contains illegal items like drugs or fake documents, then pressure them into paying a "fine" or sharing personal details to avoid legal trouble.

Full guide: What Is Courier/Parcel Scam Calls? How It Works and How to Protect Yourself

SEBI and RBI Impersonation Scams

SEBI/RBI impersonation scams involve fraudsters falsely claiming to represent SEBI or RBI — often to lend fake legitimacy to a trading scheme, or to threaten victims already caught in an investment scam into paying further "penalties" or "release fees."

How it works: Scammers create fake SEBI or RBI registration certificates, letterheads, or websites to make a fraudulent trading platform appear regulated. In a follow-up variant, once a victim has lost money to an investment scam, a second caller poses as a SEBI or RBI "official" demanding a penalty or tax payment before their money can be "released."

Warning signs: Any trading platform showing a SEBI registration number that can't be verified on the official SEBI site, and any call from someone claiming to be a SEBI or RBI official demanding payment to release funds or waive a penalty.

Protect yourself: Always verify a broker's SEBI registration directly on scores.sebi.gov.in, not via documents the platform provides. Remember SEBI and RBI never call individuals asking for payment.

Fake Electricity Bill Scams

A fake electricity bill scam is when fraudsters, posing as the local electricity board, call or message claiming an unpaid bill will lead to disconnection within hours unless payment is made immediately through a link or app they provide.

How it works: The victim receives an urgent SMS or call claiming their electricity connection will be cut off that same evening due to a pending bill, directing them to call a number or click a link to pay instantly. The link leads to a fake payment page, or the caller asks the victim to install a remote-access app to "process" the payment, through which bank details are stolen.

Warning signs: Urgency about disconnection within hours (real utilities give advance notice through official bills, not last-minute calls), a payment link outside your utility's official app or website, and any request to install a remote-access app.

Protect yourself: Pay electricity bills only through your utility's official app, website, or authorized counters. Never click links from unsolicited SMS or install any app at a caller's instruction.

Deeper guide: Electricity Bill Disconnection Scam in India: How It Works

Fake Debt Collector Scams

A fake debt collector scam is when a scammer poses as a legitimate debt collection agency, pressuring victims to pay debts that don't exist or have already been settled.

How it works: The scammer calls claiming you owe a debt, often citing an old or unfamiliar account, and uses aggressive tactics — threats of arrest or legal action — to pressure immediate payment, usually via gift card or wire transfer.

Warning signs: Threats of arrest or immediate legal action, pressure to pay via gift card or wire transfer, and refusal to provide written validation of the debt.

Protect yourself: Legitimate debt collectors must provide written validation of a debt if you request it, and real debt collection doesn't involve arrest threats. Ask for validation in writing before paying anything.

Deeper guide: How to Tell If a Debt Collector Is Legitimate

Tech Support Scams

A tech support scam is when a scammer poses as tech support (often for Microsoft or Apple) to convince a victim their device is infected, then charges for fake repairs or steals data via remote access.

How it works: A pop-up warning or unsolicited call claims your device has a virus, directing you to call a number or grant remote access. Once connected, the scammer may charge for unnecessary "repairs," install real malware, or steal personal and financial information.

Warning signs: An unsolicited pop-up or call claiming your device is infected, a request for remote access to your computer, and a request for payment via gift card or wire transfer.

Protect yourself: Real tech companies don't proactively call you about a virus. Close the pop-up, don't call the number, and never grant remote access to someone who contacted you first.

Deeper guide: Gave Remote Access in a Tech Support Scam? | Scan My Shadow

Loan App Harassment

Loan app harassment refers to predatory instant-loan apps that lend small amounts at high hidden charges, then use threats, morphed photos, and calls to the borrower's entire contact list to pressure repayment.

How it works: Victims download an app offering quick, minimal-paperwork loans, which during installation requests broad access to contacts, photos, and messages. After disbursing a small loan with steep hidden fees, recovery agents call the borrower and their contacts repeatedly, sometimes sending morphed or embarrassing images, to coerce immediate repayment.

Warning signs: An app requesting excessive permissions (contacts, gallery, messages) for a simple loan, hidden processing fees far higher than the amount disclosed, and abusive calls or messages to your contacts after taking the loan.

Protect yourself: Only borrow from RBI-registered NBFCs or banks, check an app's lender registration before installing it, and avoid apps asking for contact list or gallery access. If harassed, report it to the National Cybercrime Helpline (1930) and your local police.

Deeper guide: How Loan Apps in India Collect and Misuse Personal Data

Fake Trading App Scams

Fake trading app scams involve fraudulent investment or stock-trading apps that impersonate real brokers or promise unrealistically high, guaranteed returns, taking deposits from victims that are never actually invested.

How it works: Victims are typically approached via WhatsApp, Telegram, or social media ads promoting an "expert-guided" trading group. They're directed to download an app or use a web dashboard showing fabricated, ever-increasing profits, encouraging larger and larger deposits — until withdrawal requests are blocked or additional "fees" are demanded, and the scammers disappear.

Warning signs: Unsolicited investment tips from a WhatsApp or Telegram group, an app not listed on the Google Play Store or Apple App Store, guaranteed or unusually high returns with no risk, and withdrawal requests that get blocked or require more payment first.

Protect yourself: Only use trading apps and brokers registered with SEBI, verify any broker on the SEBI website before depositing money, and be skeptical of any group promising guaranteed or consistently high returns.

Deeper guide: Investment or Trading App Scam Recovery | Scan My Shadow

Employment Scams

An employment scam is a fake job offer designed to extract personal information, banking details, or upfront payments from job seekers.

How it works: A scammer posts an attractive, often remote job listing, conducts a quick interview (sometimes entirely by text), then extends an offer requiring the victim to pay for "equipment" or "training," or to share sensitive personal and banking information as part of onboarding.

Warning signs: An offer that comes with minimal or no real interview process, upfront payment requests, and requests for sensitive information (SSN, bank details) before any formal hiring paperwork.

Protect yourself: Research the company independently before sharing any information, be wary of jobs that require upfront payment for equipment or training, and never share your SSN or bank details before receiving formal, verifiable hiring documents.

WhatsApp Job Fraud

Job fraud via WhatsApp is when scammers message unsolicited "job offers" — often simple online tasks or part-time work — that require an upfront registration, training, or "activation" payment before any real job materializes.

How it works: A message arrives from an unknown number offering flexible, high-paying part-time work such as reviewing products, liking videos, or app-testing. After a few small tasks with token payouts to build trust, the victim is asked to pay a registration or deposit fee to "unlock" higher-paying tasks, after which the scammer stops responding or demands more payments.

Warning signs: An unsolicited job offer over WhatsApp from an unknown number, high pay for minimal effort, and any request to pay money to register, get "training," or unlock further tasks.

Protect yourself: Legitimate employers never ask you to pay to get or start a job. Do not share bank details or make any payment for an unsolicited WhatsApp job offer, and verify the company independently before engaging further.

Deeper guide: Fake Job Registration Fee? Recovery Steps | Scan My Shadow

Task-Based Part-Time Job Scams

Task-based part-time job scams recruit victims with the promise of easy pay for completing simple online tasks — such as rating products, liking videos, or subscribing to channels — while secretly using an escalating deposit structure to extract money.

How it works: After a few genuine small tasks with real payouts to build trust, victims are moved into a group and asked to "invest" increasing amounts to unlock higher-paying task batches or to "top up" a wallet balance to withdraw earnings already shown on-screen. The displayed balance is fake, and once a large enough deposit is made, the scammers stop responding.

Warning signs: Task groups on Telegram or WhatsApp offering payment per simple task, a rising "wallet balance" that requires a deposit before you can withdraw it, and pressure to add more group members for bonus tasks.

Protect yourself: Treat any task-based earning scheme that asks you to deposit money to unlock withdrawals as a scam — legitimate task or gig work never requires you to pay to get paid. Stop participating and don't add further funds if asked.

Deeper guide: Fake Job Registration Fee? Recovery Steps | Scan My Shadow

Rental Scams

A rental scam is when a scammer advertises a property (often one they don't own or that doesn't exist) to collect a deposit from a victim who never gets to move in.

How it works: The scammer copies photos and details from a real listing, posts it at an attractive price, and claims to be unavailable to show the property in person (often citing being "out of the country"), pressuring the victim to send a deposit to secure it sight unseen.

Warning signs: A price below market rate, a landlord who won't meet or video call, and pressure to send a deposit before viewing the property.

Protect yourself: Never send money for a rental before viewing it in person or via a verified video call, and try to verify the listed owner independently.

Deeper guide: Rental Listing Scams: How to Spot a Fake Apartment Ad

Timeshare Resale Scams

A timeshare resale scam targets timeshare owners with a promise to sell their unwanted timeshare quickly, collecting upfront fees for a sale that never happens.

How it works: A scammer contacts a timeshare owner claiming to have a buyer lined up, requesting an upfront fee for "closing costs" or "taxes." No sale ever occurs, and the scammer disappears with the fee.

Warning signs: An unsolicited call about your timeshare, upfront fees required before any sale, and pressure to act quickly on a supposedly ready buyer.

Protect yourself: Never pay upfront fees to sell a timeshare — legitimate resale companies are paid from the sale proceeds, not before a sale happens.

Ghost Broking

Ghost broking is when a scammer sells fake or invalid insurance policies — usually for cars — at attractively low prices, leaving the buyer uninsured.

A warranty renewal scam uses fake "final notice" mailers or robocalls about an expiring vehicle warranty to pressure victims into paying for coverage that's often worthless or nonexistent.

Paste sites let anyone publish plain text and share it with a link. Leakers have long used them to post lists of email addresses taken from breaches. The US National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) defines PII as “information that can be used to distinguish or trace an individual's identity, either alone” or when combined with other information that is linked or linkable to that person.

NIST's glossary puts it simply: metadata is “data about data,” or “information describing the characteristics of data.” If a letter is the data, the envelope (sender, recipient, postmark, date) is the metadata.

Doxxing

Doxxing is the act of researching and publicly publishing someone's private information — like their home address or phone number — usually to harass or intimidate them.

How it works: Someone compiles information about a target from public records, data broker sites, social media, and old data breaches, then publishes it online (often on forums or social media) to enable harassment or threats.

Warning signs: Increased unwanted contact after an online dispute, being able to find your own home address or phone number easily via a search, and your information appearing on data broker sites.

Protect yourself: Regularly check what's publicly findable about you, opt out of data broker sites that list your address and phone number, and tighten privacy settings on social media.

Five rules that stop most scams

  1. Never share a one-time code, PIN or CVV. Banks, payment apps and government agencies do not ask for them by phone, text or chat.
  2. Treat urgency as a warning sign. Threats of arrest, account blocking or disconnection "today" are designed to stop you from checking.
  3. Call back on a number you already trust. Use the number on your card, bill or the official website, never one given in the message or call.
  4. Do not install apps or give remote access because someone asked. Screen-sharing and remote-access apps let a stranger see and control your phone or computer.
  5. Report fast if money has moved. In India call 1930 or use cybercrime.gov.in; elsewhere contact your bank first, then your national fraud reporting service.
