=== TLDR
Free reverse phone lookups can sometimes name a business or show spam reports, but for personal mobiles most “free” sites give a teaser and push a paid report, often with outdated data. And if the call was spoofed, any lookup points to an innocent person. The most reliable free steps: search the number in quotes, check your carrier's spam labels, let unknown calls go to voicemail, and never call back numbers that pressure you. Phone lookup searches run in the hundreds of thousands a month in the US, so it's a big business.

=== BODY
## Why everyone is googling numbers

Phone lookup is one of the most-searched privacy topics in the US. Google's Keyword Planner puts the main “phone lookup” and “reverse phone lookup” terms in the 100,000 to 1 million monthly searches range. That's a lot of people staring at a missed call from a number they don't know.

## What the free options really do

| Method | What it can tell you | Catch |
|---|---|---|
| Search the number in quotes on Google or Bing | Businesses, public listings, complaint pages | Personal mobiles rarely show up |
| Your carrier's caller ID / spam label | “Spam likely” or a business name | Labels can lag behind new scam numbers |
| Free people-search sites (TruePeopleSearch, USPhonebook, etc.) | A name and address tied to the number | Data can be years out of date; it's also *your* data on show |
| “Free” lookup sites with paid reports | A teaser (city, carrier) then a paywall | The “free” part is the advert |
| Apps that crowdsource spam reports | Community warnings | Some apps upload your contacts to build their database |

## The spoofing problem

Scammers routinely fake caller ID. The [FCC](https://fcc.gov/spoofing) describes “neighbor spoofing”, where the number looks local or similar to yours. Look up a spoofed number and you'll find an ordinary person who has no idea their number was used. Please don't ring them up angry.

That's also why a lookup can't tell you whether a call claiming to be your bank or the IRS is real. Only calling the official number yourself can. Read [[what-is-caller-id-spoofing|how spoofing works]].

## A safer routine for unknown calls

1. Let it go to voicemail. Real callers leave messages.
2. Search the number in quotes, plus words like “scam” or “complaint.”
3. If it claims to be a company or agency, hang up and call the number on your statement or the official website.
4. Don't press buttons to “stop calls.” The FCC says to avoid that and yes/no questions.
5. Report scam calls to the [FTC](https://reportfraud.ftc.gov/) and register on the [[national-do-not-call-registry-guide|Do Not Call Registry]].

If others can look up a number and get a name and address, so can anyone with yours. === FAQ
Q: Is there a truly free reverse phone lookup?
A: Searching the number in quotes and your carrier's spam labels are free. Many "free" lookup sites show a teaser and charge for details.
Q: Why does a reverse lookup show the wrong person?
A: The number may have been spoofed, recycled to a new owner, or the site's data may be out of date.
Q: Can I find out who owns a mobile number?
A: Sometimes, through people-search sites, but results are often outdated and those sites list your information too.
Q: Should I call back an unknown number?
A: Not if it pressured you or claimed to be a bank or agency. Call the official number instead.
