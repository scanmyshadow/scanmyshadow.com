=== TLDR
If your authenticator app keeps asking you to approve a sign-in you didn't start, someone almost certainly **has your password** and is **"push bombing"** you, hoping you tap Approve to make it stop. It's how a hacker got into **Uber in 2022**: after more than an hour of prompts, they messaged the victim on WhatsApp pretending to be IT. **Deny every prompt**, **change that password now**, and switch to **number matching**, a **passkey** or a **security key**. Microsoft made number matching mandatory in its Authenticator app from **8 May 2023**, and CISA calls phishing-resistant MFA the best defence.

=== BODY
## What MFA fatigue is

Two-factor authentication works because a password alone isn't enough. MFA fatigue (also called **push bombing**) is how attackers get around it. According to [CISA](https://www.cisa.gov/sites/default/files/publications/fact-sheet-implement-number-matching-in-mfa-applications-508c.pdf), an attacker who already has your password repeatedly triggers push notifications, hoping you approve one by accident or just to make them stop.

It's a war of attrition: 2 a.m., twentieth notification, half asleep, thumb hovering over Approve. That's the moment they're waiting for.

## How it worked against Uber

[TechCrunch reported](https://techcrunch.com/2022/09/19/how-to-fix-another-uber-breach) how a hacker linked to the Lapsus$ group got into Uber in September 2022:

1. They had a **contractor's password**, possibly bought on a dark web marketplace or stolen by password-stealing malware.
2. They sent **push prompts repeatedly for over an hour**.
3. Then they messaged the contractor **on WhatsApp**, claiming to be **Uber IT**, and said that if he wanted the prompts to stop, he had to accept one.
4. He accepted, and the hacker **added their own device**, giving them ongoing access.

Notice the second half. The prompts wear you down; the fake "IT support" message tells you what to do. **Real IT staff will never ask you to approve a prompt you didn't start.**

## A prompt means your password is out

CISA notes that MFA is checked **after** the password, so an unexpected approval request usually means your password is already compromised. Those prompts aren't just annoying. They're an alarm.

## What to do right now

1. **Deny every prompt you didn't start.** Never approve "just to see."
2. **Ignore anyone who contacts you about the prompts**, by phone, text or WhatsApp, even if they say they're IT or the company's security team.
3. **Change the password** for that account immediately, and anywhere you reused it.
4. **Check sign-in activity**, remove unknown devices and sign out of other sessions ([[how-to-log-out-of-all-devices|how to log out of all devices]]).
5. **If it's a work account**, tell your IT or security team right away.
6. **Check whether the password leaked** in a breach ([[check-if-your-password-has-been-leaked|how]]).

## Settings that stop it

| Fix | How it helps | Where |
|---|---|---|
| **Number matching** | You must type a number shown on the login screen into your app. An attacker who can't see your screen can't approve | Microsoft Authenticator (mandatory since 8 May 2023), Duo, Okta |
| **Passkeys** | Sign-in is tied to your device and the real website, so there's nothing to approve remotely | Google, Apple, Microsoft and many others; see [[setting-up-passkeys-and-authenticator|setting up passkeys]] |
| **Security keys** | Physical keys that can't be phished; [[password-manager-mfa-security-key-guide|how they work]] | Most major accounts |
| **Unique passwords** | One breach doesn't unlock everything | A password manager |

According to [BleepingComputer](https://www.bleepingcomputer.com/news/microsoft/microsoft-enforces-number-matching-to-fight-mfa-fatigue-attacks/amp/), Microsoft removed the admin option to turn number matching off and enforced it for all Authenticator push users, calling it "a key security upgrade." CISA's [guidance](https://cisa.gov/news-events/alerts/2022/10/31/cisa-releases-guidance-phishing-resistant-and-numbers-matching) recommends **phishing-resistant MFA** as the strongest option and number matching as an interim step.

## For businesses

TechCrunch notes that security teams can also **cap** how many push prompts a user can receive and **alert** when that limit is hit. Combined with number matching, that turns a push-bombing attempt into an early warning instead of a breach.

## Was it a mistake?

Occasionally an old device or app retries a login with a saved password. If prompts arrive in a steady trickle at the same time daily, check your other devices. A sudden burst at odd hours is almost certainly an attack.

## Where did they get your password?

Usually from an older breach, a phishing page or password-stealing malware. A free [[/digital-footprint-checker/|email exposure check]] shows which breaches your email appears in, which often explains which password was stolen. Then read up on [[credential-stuffing-explained|credential stuffing]], the attack that reuses those leaked passwords.

=== FAQ
Q: What is MFA fatigue?
A: An attack where someone with your password floods you with login approval requests, hoping you tap Approve by mistake or to make them stop.
Q: Why am I getting login approval requests I didn't make?
A: Someone probably has your password. Deny the requests and change the password immediately.
Q: Someone from IT messaged me to approve the prompt. Should I?
A: No. In the 2022 Uber breach, the attacker posed as IT on WhatsApp to get the victim to approve. Contact your IT team through a channel you already know.
Q: What is number matching?
A: A sign-in method where you type a number shown on the login screen into your authenticator app. Microsoft made it mandatory in Authenticator from 8 May 2023.
Q: What's the best protection against MFA fatigue?
A: Phishing-resistant MFA such as passkeys or security keys, plus unique passwords from a password manager.
