=== TLDR
In early October 2026 ASOS app users got a push notification reading “ASOS HACKED”, sent by attackers. ASOS confirmed that third-party platforms it uses to talk to customers were accessed and that names and contact details may be exposed. Samples given to the BBC reportedly also include addresses, phone numbers, dates of birth, customer numbers and the searches people made on ASOS. Passwords and card details were not accessed, ASOS says. Expect very personal-sounding phishing.

=== BODY
## What happened

On a Tuesday morning in October 2026 ASOS shoppers received an in-app alert that simply said “ASOS HACKED”, addressed to the company's data protection officer: “we have fully compromised the Snowflake instance. Engage with us, or we will leak it.” [BleepingComputer](https://www.bleepingcomputer.com/news/security/asos-confirms-data-breach-after-hacked-in-app-notifications/) reports that the message pointed to a Telegram channel run by a group calling itself “Xuanye.”

ASOS confirmed that third-party platforms it uses to communicate with customers had been accessed without authorisation, and said basic personal information, including names and contact details, may have been exposed. It told customers to ignore the rogue notification and not to click its link.

## How they got in

According to [Malwarebytes](https://www.malwarebytes.com/blog/data-breaches/2026/10/asos-breach-update-hackers-stole-customer-details-and-shopping-searches), ASOS says attackers tricked an employee into handing over login details, then used them to reach third-party platforms. The attackers first blamed Snowflake, which said its own platform was not compromised. They later claimed access came through a personalisation tool built on Snowflake.

## What may have been taken

Per the BBC sample reported by Malwarebytes:

- names, home addresses, phone numbers and email addresses;
- customer numbers and dates of birth;
- **searches made on the ASOS site** (for example “glamorous wide fit”);
- when each customer started shopping with ASOS.

ASOS says payment card details and passwords were not accessed. The number of customers affected has not been published, and the attackers reportedly demanded a ransom within two weeks.

## Why the search history part matters

Contact details leak all the time. Shopping searches are different. A phishing email that says “Your saved search for petite wide-leg jeans has a 70% discount, confirm your address to claim” feels like it came from ASOS because it knows things only ASOS should know. That is the risk here: not your card, but how convincing the next fake message can be.

## What to do now

1. **Ignore any message about the breach, refunds or compensation** that arrives by email, text or call. ASOS says it will contact affected customers directly and will never ask for passwords, codes or card details.
2. **Change your ASOS password anyway**, and anywhere you reused it. Here's [[check-if-your-password-has-been-leaked|how to check if a password has leaked]].
3. **Turn on two-factor authentication** on your email first, because your email resets everything else. See [[how-to-set-up-two-factor-authentication|setting up 2FA]].
4. **Watch for delivery-themed texts.** Fake “your ASOS parcel is held” messages are an easy follow-up, see [[evri-delivery-text-scam-what-to-do|Evri text scams]] and [[royal-mail-delivery-text-scam-what-to-do|Royal Mail text scams]].
5. **Report phishing**: forward emails to report@phishing.gov.uk and texts to 7726.

## Where else has your email been?

If you shop online, your email has almost certainly been in more than one breach. If you think you have lost money, report it to [Report Fraud](https://www.reportfraud.police.uk/) and see our guide to [[how-to-report-a-scam-to-action-fraud-uk|reporting fraud in the UK]].
